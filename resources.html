AI and automation, in plain language.

A short guide to what AI and automation can actually do for a small company, how you get started, and what the words mean. Written for an owner who is not a developer. You do not have to read all of it, and you do not have to learn anything to use it.

3 chapters + a glossary No sign-up
Chapter 1

What it can actually do.

AI is not one thing. It is a lot of different tools that share a characteristic: they can do something that requires judgement, because they have learned patterns instead of calculating. That is why they are good at what is repetitive and clear, and poor at anything that needs you to notice whether a result is correct. Here are four places where it works.

01

Keeping the customer

A welcome email, a follow-up after a purchase, a reminder about an abandoned basket. That kind of thing runs in the background all the time, and it is often the fastest thing to get a return on. A welcome sequence alone can recover 10-15 percent of the baskets that would otherwise be forgotten.

02

Answering the same thing again and again

Opening hours, prices, shipping, returns. Those questions can be answered around the clock. When a question is too hard, it goes to a person. Most businesses find that 50-70 percent of ordinary questions can be answered automatically.

03

Finding patterns in the numbers

Which products sell together, when your customers buy, which channel actually brings paying customers, and who is about to leave. Those are the questions that end up in a spreadsheet and get answered by feel.

04

Getting past the blank page

Posts, newsletters, product descriptions. Not to replace your voice, but to get past the blank page. You give it your ideas and your style, and you get a draft you can edit. Three hours of writing becomes half an hour of editing.

Chapter 2

How to get started, in five steps.

This is the order I would follow if it were my own company. Start with one thing. Get it running. Then move on.

Step 01

Find one task you do every day

Not the whole department. One task. The one you do because you did not choose it. When that runs, you have a finished thing you can show, and a feeling for what it cost. That feeling is what makes the next one happen.

Step 02

Connect your tools

You have a CRM, mail, a calendar, a shop, a stock system. When they talk to each other, something happens automatically that you would otherwise have to remember: a new customer enters the CRM, gets an email, and a task lands in the calendar. It does not require a developer. It requires a willingness to draw lines between the fields.

Step 03

Put an AI layer on top

Once data is moving, AI can make it sensible. An assistant that reads your FAQ and answers in plain language. An email written from what the customer bought. A dashboard that summarises the week and tells you what to look at. This is where the difference between automated and merely automated appears.

Step 04

Build on, one at a time

Automate lead capture from web to CRM. Get it running reliably. Then automate invoice reminders. Then customer satisfaction surveys. Each one saves a few hours a month, and after half a year those hours are a working week.

Step 05

Keep it secure

The more tools talk to each other, the more data moves around. Use a password manager. Turn on two-factor authentication wherever it exists. Take backups. And never share API keys or passwords — treat access to your system the way you treat access to your bank account.

Glossary

The words you will run into.

No prior knowledge assumed. If you need a definition that is not here, ask — it is not a silly question.

LLM

Large Language Model. A model trained on large amounts of text that can understand, summarise and write. Think of a very well-read assistant who can write, explain and answer.

API

A digital handshake between two programs. When your CRM talks to your mail tool, it happens through an API. It is what lets tools work together as one system.

Automation

Getting a computer to do a repetitive task so you do not have to. Like a rule: "when a new customer signs up, send a welcome email and create them in the CRM".

Agent

An AI program that can act on its own — answer questions, write reports, analyse data. A chatbot replies. An agent acts.

RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation. A method where the AI looks up your own documents before it answers. That means the answer is based on your business rather than on general knowledge.

Embedding

A way of turning words into numbers, so a computer can work out that "winter coat" and "warm jacket" mean something similar. It is the basis of a search that understands what you actually mean.

Vector database

A database that stores information by meaning rather than by keyword. That is how it finds the right document even when you used different words than the original.

Workflow

A sequence of automatic steps that happen in order. "Form submitted → customer created in CRM → welcome email sent → task added to calendar".

Pipeline

A series of connected steps that data flows through. A lead pipeline can move someone from "visitor" to "subscriber" to "meeting booked" to "customer" without you touching it.

Dashboard

One screen with the numbers that matter, gathered in one place. Sales today, new leads, open tasks. Instead of checking five tools you look at one. Like the instrument panel in a car.

Prompt

The instruction you give the AI. A good prompt is specific. Instead of "write about my service" you can write "write three paragraphs about my garden waste removal, aimed at homeowners over 60, with a free estimate offer".

Fine-tuning

Training a general model further on your own data until it becomes a specialist in your field. Like taking a capable new hire who knows a bit of everything and teaching them everything about your specific business.
